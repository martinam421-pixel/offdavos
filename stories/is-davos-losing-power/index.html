IS DAVOS LOSING POWER?

Maybe that's the wrong question.

For one week, an unusual number of CEOs, politicians, investors, journalists and institutions still gather publicly in one tiny mountain town.

The circus is changing.

That doesn't necessarily mean it disappeared.

First: WEF is not Davos

This sounds obvious. It isn't.

The World Economic Forum is the organisation. Davos is the place.

And during WEF week, those two things increasingly feel like separate worlds.

There is the official Annual Meeting inside the Congress Centre: heads of state, CEOs, panels, speeches, security and a badge system that determines which parts of it you can access.

And then there is everything happening around it.

The Promenade. Hotel lobbies. Private dinners. Company houses. Investor breakfasts. Founder events. Media events. Crypto events. AI events. Events about events.

And people standing outside all of them trying to figure out whether somebody knows somebody who can get them in.

The badge economy

Davos has its own class system. Conveniently, you can wear it around your neck.

Badges determine where you can go, which parts of the official meeting you can enter and, to some extent, how interesting you suddenly become to strangers.

But here's the strange thing: you can spend an entire week in Davos without ever entering the Congress Centre.

Because the official programme is only one layer of what happens here.

CEOs are in hotel lobbies. Investors are at breakfasts. Founders are running events. Journalists are everywhere.

The badge matters. But Davos doesn't end where the badge stops working.

And then there are the prices

Davos during WEF operates under a different interpretation of economics.

Accommodation becomes scarce. Venues become scarce. Tables become scarce. Basically anything involving four walls starts behaving like an asset class.

During WEF 2026, Blick reported that one Davos restaurant was charging CHF 51 for a spaghetti dish with beef fillet strips, grilled vegetables and spicy tomato sauce.

At some point, you find yourself staring at CHF 51 spaghetti and wondering whether the pasta knows something you don't.

But focusing only on the absurd prices misses something important.

Because the absurdity is real. The money is real. The exclusivity is real. And people keep coming.

Which brings us to the actual question

There is a real debate about whether the World Economic Forum itself still has the influence it once had.

That's a fair question.

What does a global forum actually achieve? How important is the official programme? How much of the real value comes from the panels — and how much comes from putting an extraordinary concentration of powerful people in the same small town at the same time?

Then you look at the numbers.

According to the World Economic Forum, nearly 3,000 participants from 130 countries attended its 2026 Annual Meeting. That included close to 65 heads of state and government, around 400 senior political leaders, and close to 830 CEOs and chairs.

Those are not the numbers of an event nobody cares about.

So perhaps something more complicated is happening.

So is Davos losing power?

Maybe that's the wrong question.

The World Economic Forum itself can face questions about its relevance while Davos remains extraordinarily good at attracting people who want to meet other people with power, money, influence, information or access.

Those aren't contradictory ideas.

And increasingly, not everything important in Davos happens inside the official programme.

The Promenade fills with temporary houses. Companies host their own programmes. Countries create their own spaces. Communities organise dinners. Investors hold breakfasts.

Some require the right badge. Some require the right invitation. Some are considerably easier to enter.

The result is something bigger and messier than a single conference.

The WEF remains at the centre of the week. But it no longer owns every room in which Davos happens.

Maybe the shift isn't that Davos is losing power. Maybe some of that power is becoming less concentrated in the Forum itself.

And if that's true, it isn't necessarily a bad thing.

Because participation doesn't always begin at the Congress Centre.

You don't always need the most important badge. You don't always need to be on the official programme.

Sometimes you can create something alongside it. Sometimes you can invite your own people. Sometimes you can start your own conversation.

Sometimes you can build your own room.
